## Part A: Top 40 AI-consulting interview questions with model answers

### Theme 1: Concepts

**Q1. Explain the difference between machine learning, generative AI and agentic AI to a COO in two minutes.**

Three layers, each adding a capability. Machine learning learns a mapping from historical data to a prediction or classification: fraud score, default probability, next best offer. Its output is a number or label, and it is well understood by model risk functions under SR 11-7 style guidance [PUBLIC — Federal Reserve, 2011]. Generative AI, built on large language models, produces content, text, code, structured extractions, from a prompt and context; it reasons over language rather than fitting a target variable, which makes it good at unstructured work and inherently probabilistic in its errors. Agentic AI wraps a model in a loop with tools, memory and planning so it can take actions towards a goal across several steps: query a system, decide, call another system, ask a human. The COO's takeaway: ML predicts, GenAI produces, agents act. Each step up adds value and adds a new class of risk, from wrong predictions to wrong content to wrong actions, so the control design must step up with it.

**Q2. What is a context window and why does it matter commercially?**

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can attend to in one call, covering system prompt, retrieved documents, conversation history and the output. Commercially it matters in three ways. Cost: you pay per token, so every document you stuff into context is a recurring charge; a 50,000-token KYB case file processed once per step across a ten-step workflow is 500,000 input tokens per case. Quality: attention degrades with very long inputs, the "lost in the middle" effect, so bigger is not automatically better. Architecture: the window size decides whether you need retrieval-augmented generation, summarisation or memory structures. The consultant point is that context is a design variable with a price tag. At frontier pricing of the order of USD 2–5 per million input tokens [ESTIMATE — order of magnitude, VERIFY], the KYB case above costs around USD 1–2.50 in input alone before caching, which is why prompt caching and model tiering appear in every serious TCO.

**Q3. What is RAG, and when is it the wrong answer?**

Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020 [PUBLIC]) retrieves relevant passages from an indexed corpus, typically via embeddings and a vector store, and places them in the model's context so the answer is grounded in the organisation's own documents rather than the model's parametric memory. It is the default pattern for policy, procedure and knowledge questions because it is cheaper and more controllable than fine-tuning and gives you citations for audit. It is the wrong answer in three situations. When the task needs the model to behave differently rather than know more, for example a consistent output format or tone, fine-tuning or structured prompting is the lever. When the corpus is small enough to fit the context, retrieval adds latency and failure points for nothing. And when the question requires computation or live system data, retrieval of static text is the wrong tool; you need tool calling. A junior mistake is to propose RAG for every use case; a manager asks what the failure mode is, retrieval miss or generation error, and designs for it.

**Q4. Why do LLMs hallucinate, and what actually reduces it?**

A language model generates the most probable continuation given its context; it has no internal notion of truth, only of plausibility. Hallucination arises when the probable continuation is not the correct one: the model has no relevant knowledge, the retrieved context is missing or contradictory, or the prompt invites confident completion. Four mitigations work, in descending order of effect. Grounding: give the model the facts via retrieval or tools and instruct it to answer only from them, with citations. Constraint: restrict the output to structured schemas and enumerated options, which turns open generation into selection. Verification: a second pass that checks claims against sources, or a deterministic rule that validates fields. Human review: sampled or exception-based, proportionate to error cost. What does not work on its own is "better prompting" as a control; it reduces frequency but is not auditable. In banking, the standard a model risk function will accept is measured groundedness on a golden set, with a defined threshold and a monitoring process.

**Q5. Frontier model or small open-weight model: how do you choose?**

Decide on five criteria. Capability on your evaluation set, not on public benchmarks: run the golden set through both. Cost per task at expected volume: small models can be ten to fifty times cheaper per token [ESTIMATE — order of magnitude, VERIFY], which dominates at high volume for narrow tasks. Latency: sub-second requirements, as in payment authorisation paths, generally exclude large models. Data residency and control: open-weight models can be hosted in the bank's own environment, which some risk functions and regulators prefer. Operational burden: self-hosting shifts patching, scaling and safety tuning onto the bank. The usual answer is a tiered design: a frontier model for the few judgement-heavy steps, small models for high-volume classification and extraction, with a router. The consultant framing is that model choice is a portfolio decision made per task, not a one-time vendor choice.

### Theme 2: Frameworks

**Q6. How do you assess an organisation's AI maturity?**

Use a multi-dimensional model, score each dimension on a defined scale and compare with the stated ambition. Dimensions should cover strategy and leadership ambition, data foundations, technology platform, talent and operating model, governance and risk, and value delivery and adoption. Gartner publishes an AI maturity model with five levels [PUBLIC — Gartner; VERIFY current version], and McKinsey's "Rewired" (Lamarre, Smaje and Zemmel, 2023 [PUBLIC]) frames six capabilities: business-led digital roadmap, talent, operating model, technology, data, and adoption and scaling. The method matters more than the brand. Evidence each score with artefacts, not interviews alone: a data catalogue, a model inventory, a funded roadmap. Then set the ambition explicitly, defend, extend or transform, because a bank aiming to defend its cost position needs a different maturity target from one aiming to reinvent servicing. The output is a gap-to-ambition view that becomes the enabler workstream in the roadmap. The mistake is to produce a maturity score without a decision attached to it.

**Q7. Explain BCG's 10-20-70 rule and how you use it.**

BCG's heuristic [PUBLIC — BCG, 2023–24] says that in AI programmes roughly 10% of the effort and value is in algorithms and models, 20% in technology and data, and 70% in people, process and business change. I use it in three places. In the business case, to sanity-check the cost split: a programme budget that is 70% model licences and 10% change is inverted and will under-deliver. In the operating model, to argue for change capacity, process redesign and adoption measurement as first-class workstreams. In vendor conversations, to push back on propositions that price the model and hand-wave the integration and adoption. The caveat I add: the ratio is a heuristic about effort, not a formula, and in regulated banking the governance and validation share is larger than the rule implies. Pair it with McKinsey's "Rewired" capabilities for the fuller picture of what the 70% consists of.

**Q8. Walk me through how you build and prioritise a use-case portfolio.**

Five steps. Discovery: walk the value chain, decompose processes to task level, and build a long list with pain points from SMEs and data. Cleaning: de-duplicate, cluster into domains, and write each use case as a problem statement with a value hypothesis. Assessment: score value, across cost, revenue, risk and customer experience, and feasibility, across data readiness, integration complexity, model maturity, regulatory exposure and change effort, with weights agreed with the sponsor before scoring. Overlay: a risk tier and a dependency map, because the top-scoring use case is often blocked by a data foundation nobody scored. Sequencing: waves, with quick wins to fund credibility, foundational investments to unlock the next wave, and transformational bets that need the foundations. Value-vs-feasibility matrices are generic tools; RICE (Intercom) and ICE (Sean Ellis) are product-management variants [PUBLIC]. The manager-level point is that weights are a political decision, so agree them in the room with the sponsor and the risk function.

**Q9. How do you assess whether a process step is suited to an agent?**

Score the step, not the process. The framework I use has six dimensions: task variability (does the input vary in form and content, which rules cannot handle), judgement required (interpretation versus lookup), system and tool access (can the agent actually act), error tolerance and reversibility (what a wrong action costs and whether it can be undone), data availability (is the evidence machine-readable), and volume and frequency (does the economics justify the build). Attribution: a practitioner framework, variants differ by firm. Deterministic, low-variability steps go to rules or integrations; high-variability, high-judgement, reversible steps are agent candidates; irreversible, high-consequence decisions keep a human. In a KYB assessment [ILLUSTRATIVE — composite] about a third of steps typically score as rules, half as agent-fit with review, and a handful, such as final risk rating and adverse decisions, stay human. The scope that falls out is the business case.

**Q10. What is "Deploy, Reshape, Invent" and why does the distinction matter?**

It is BCG's framing of three levels of GenAI ambition [PUBLIC — BCG, 2023–24]. Deploy: adopt available tools to raise individual productivity, such as coding assistants and document drafting, with low integration and quick but diffuse value. Reshape: redesign end-to-end processes and functions around AI, such as agentic onboarding or claims, where the value is large and the change effort is where the risk sits. Invent: create new products, services or business models that AI makes possible. It matters because each needs a different funding model, governance posture and success metric. Deploy value is measured in adoption and time saved and is routinely overstated because saved minutes do not become saved cost. Reshape value is measured in unit cost and cycle time and requires process ownership. Invent value is measured in revenue and option value and needs venture-style gates. A portfolio should be explicit about which of the three each initiative is.

### Theme 3: Agentic AI

**Q11. Describe the main agent architectures and when you would use each.**

Single ReAct agent (Yao et al., 2022 [PUBLIC]): one model reasons and acts in a loop with tools; suitable for bounded tasks with few tools. Plan-and-execute: a planner decomposes the goal into steps, executors carry them out; better for long tasks where you want the plan reviewable before action. Supervisor or hierarchical: an orchestrator routes work to specialist agents with narrow scopes and tool lists; the pattern I recommend for regulated operations because it gives model risk one accountable control point, allows specialists to be evaluated in isolation, and keeps each agent's prompt and tools small enough to constrain behaviour. Orchestrator-worker with parallel fan-out: for independent sub-tasks such as evidence gathering from several sources. Multi-agent collaboration or debate: agents critique each other; useful for quality on high-stakes drafting, costly in tokens and hard to explain. The choice follows accountability and testability, not novelty.

**Q12. What is the Model Context Protocol, and should a bank care?**

MCP, released by Anthropic in November 2024 [PUBLIC], is an open protocol that standardises how models connect to tools and data sources: a server exposes tools, resources and prompts, and any MCP-capable client can use them without bespoke integration code. Google's Agent2Agent protocol (April 2025, donated to the Linux Foundation in June 2025 [PUBLIC; VERIFY adoption]) addresses the adjacent problem of agents communicating with other agents. A bank should care for three reasons. Integration cost: tool integration is usually the largest build cost, and standard interfaces reduce it and the lock-in. Security surface: every exposed tool is a new access path and must be governed, scoped and logged like an API. Strategy: the protocol layer is where platform choices become reversible or not. My advice is to adopt the standards for internal tool exposure with the same access controls as APIs, and to treat agent-to-agent interoperability as emerging and worth piloting rather than betting on. Verify the current state before citing adoption figures.

**Q13. How do you decide the autonomy level of an agent?**

Use an explicit scale and tie it to error cost. A workable scale is: level 0, human does the task with AI information; level 1, AI recommends, human decides and acts; level 2, AI acts, human approves before effect; level 3, AI acts, human reviews a sample after the fact; level 4, AI acts with exception-only escalation; level 5, full autonomy. Attribution: practitioner scale, variants exist. Assign the level per action, not per system, using error tolerance and reversibility, regulatory consequence, and measured accuracy on a golden set. A dispute-resolution agent might sit at level 4 for evidence assembly, level 3 for low-value provisional credits, level 2 for write-offs above a threshold and level 1 for any customer-facing adverse decision [ILLUSTRATIVE — composite]. Autonomy then becomes a dial you move up as evidence accumulates, which is how you talk about it to a regulator: earned, measured and reversible.

**Q14. What guardrails does an agentic system in a bank need?**

Four layers. Input guardrails: prompt-injection detection, data classification checks and scope filters so the agent only receives what it should. Output guardrails: schema validation, groundedness checks against sources, toxicity and PII filters, and confidence thresholds that route low-confidence outputs to humans. Action guardrails: allow-lists of tools and operations, value and volume limits, idempotency and rollback, and mandatory approval gates on irreversible actions. Policy and governance guardrails: logging of every reasoning step and tool call, model and prompt versioning, kill switches, and periodic re-validation under the model risk framework. The key design principle is that guardrails must be enforced outside the model, in deterministic code, because a model-based check can be manipulated by the same inputs that compromise the model. In interview, say that explicitly; it separates people who have built one from people who have read about them.

**Q15. When is agentic AI the wrong tool?**

Four cases. When the task is deterministic: reason-code validity, timeframe checks, eligibility rules, where a rules engine is cheaper, faster and auditable, and an agent only adds probabilistic error. When a single model call suffices: summarisation, classification and extraction do not need a loop, tools or memory, and wrapping them in an agent adds cost and failure modes. When the error cost is high and the action is irreversible: there the correct design is decision support at autonomy level 1, not an agent. When the data is not machine-readable or the systems have no interfaces: an agent with nothing to call is a chatbot. The honest framing for a client is that agents are the right tool for a narrow band of high-variability, judgement-heavy, reversible, well-instrumented work, and that most of a bank's processes contain only a few such steps. Gartner's June 2025 projection that over 40% of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by 2027 [PUBLIC — Gartner, 2025; VERIFY] is largely a statement about this misfit.

### Theme 4: Business case and TCO

**Q16. What goes into the TCO of a GenAI or agentic system?**

Four buckets over at least three years. Build: data preparation and access, integration with core systems, engineering, construction of evaluation sets, security review and model-risk validation. Run: inference and token costs, platform and infrastructure, observability and monitoring, ongoing evaluation, guardrail services, and human review capacity, which is often the largest run cost and the most often omitted. Change: process redesign, training, role changes and adoption management, the 70% in BCG's heuristic [PUBLIC — BCG, 2023–24]. Refresh: model upgrades and re-validation, prompt and retrieval re-tuning when models change, and drift monitoring. The common errors are to model inference as the dominant cost, when it is typically 10–25% of three-year TCO for an operations use case [ESTIMATE — assumption], and to omit human review and re-validation. The term TCO comes from Gartner in 1987 [PUBLIC]; the principle, count every cost of owning not just acquiring, is what a CFO will hold you to.

**Q17. How do you estimate inference cost for a use case?**

Bottom up, per task. Define the workflow steps that call a model; for each, estimate input tokens (system prompt, retrieved context, conversation state) and output tokens; multiply by the model's price per million tokens; sum across steps; multiply by annual volume. Then apply the levers: prompt caching for repeated system and context prefixes, typically 75–90% off cached input [ESTIMATE — order of magnitude, VERIFY]; batch processing for non-urgent work, around 50% off; and model tiering, routing high-volume simple steps to small models. Worked [ILLUSTRATIVE — composite]: a dispute agent with six model steps averaging 6,000 input and 600 output tokens per step is 36,000 input and 3,600 output tokens per case. At USD 3 per million input and USD 15 per million output that is about USD 0.11 input plus USD 0.05 output, roughly USD 0.16 per case; at 600,000 cases a year, about USD 96,000 before caching. The point to make: inference is rarely the line that breaks the case; integration and review are.

**Q18. How does a CFO challenge an AI business case, and how do you pre-empt it?**

Eight challenges recur. Baseline inflation: pre-empt with a measured, dated baseline and source. Capacity release versus cost: CFOs discount "hours saved" at 30–70% [ESTIMATE — practitioner range] unless tied to a headcount plan, vacancy freeze or volume absorption; state the conversion mechanism. Ramp optimism: show adoption and accuracy curves, not a step change. Double counting: reconcile with other programmes claiming the same FTE. Run-cost omission: show the full TCO including human review and re-validation. Attribution: define the counterfactual and the measurement method up front. Risk-adjustment: probability-weight benefits by evidence level, for example 90% for measured pilot results, 50% for analogues, 25% for hypotheses. Terminal assumptions: no benefit beyond the horizon unless contracted. The strongest pre-emption is a benefits register with an owner, a baseline, a measurement method and a tracking cadence for each line.

**Q19. Explain NPV, IRR and payback and when each misleads.**

NPV discounts all future net cash flows to today at the organisation's hurdle rate: NPV = Σ CF_t / (1+r)^t minus initial investment. Positive means the programme creates value at that hurdle. IRR is the discount rate at which NPV is zero; it is a return measure that is intuitive but misleading when cash flows change sign more than once or when comparing projects of different scale. Payback is the time to recover the investment; it ignores everything after payback and ignores discounting, but CFOs like it because it measures exposure. For AI programmes, NPV at the bank's hurdle, often 8–12% [ESTIMATE — practitioner range], is the decision metric; payback under 24 months is usually needed for sponsorship; IRR is reported for comparison. The specific AI distortion is a benefit ramp that back-loads value, which makes payback look worse than NPV suggests; show both and explain why.

**Q20. How do you risk-adjust an AI business case?**

Three mechanisms, applied transparently. Evidence-weighted benefits: tier each benefit line by evidence quality, measured in a pilot, observed at an analogue, or hypothesis, and apply probability weights such as 0.9, 0.5 and 0.25 [ESTIMATE — practitioner practice]. Scenario analysis: build low, base and high cases that vary the three or four assumptions that drive most of the value, typically adoption rate, accuracy at autonomy threshold, volume growth and run cost, and show NPV for each. Sensitivity: a tornado chart ranking assumptions by their effect on NPV, which tells the CFO what to monitor. Add a contingency on costs, commonly 15–25% on build [ESTIMATE — practitioner range], and a delay scenario, because regulated deployments slip on validation. Present the risk-adjusted NPV as the headline and the unadjusted as context. CFOs trust an advisor who discounts their own numbers before being asked.

### Theme 5: Governance and regulation

**Q21. How does the EU AI Act affect a bank's AI programme?**

The Act [PUBLIC — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, in force 1 Aug 2024; VERIFY current timeline] is risk-based. Prohibited practices applied from February 2025. Obligations for general-purpose AI model providers applied from August 2025. Most high-risk obligations under Annex III were scheduled for August 2026, and the Commission's November 2025 digital omnibus proposal would delay some of them, so verify the current dates. For banks, Annex III lists creditworthiness assessment of natural persons and life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing as high-risk, which brings risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, and conformity requirements. Most operations use cases, KYB, disputes, AML triage, are not Annex III high-risk, but they carry transparency obligations where people interact with AI, and they still sit under the bank's model risk and conduct frameworks. The consultant's job is to classify every use case against the Act early, because classification determines cost and timeline.

**Q22. Compare NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and PRA SS1/23.**

They operate at different levels. NIST AI RMF 1.0 [PUBLIC — Jan 2023] is a voluntary framework with four functions, Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, plus a Generative AI Profile [PUBLIC — NIST AI 600-1, July 2024]; it is a vocabulary and a checklist for risk practice. ISO/IEC 42001 [PUBLIC — Dec 2023] is a certifiable management-system standard, like ISO 27001 for security; it specifies the organisational system for AI governance and can be audited. PRA SS1/23 [PUBLIC — published May 2023, effective May 2024; VERIFY scope] is supervisory expectation for UK banks on model risk management, with five principles: model identification and risk classification, governance, development and implementation and use, independent validation, and risk mitigants. In practice a UK bank uses SS1/23 to decide what counts as a model and how to tier and validate it, ISO 42001 to structure the management system if it wants certification, and NIST to shape the risk taxonomy. GenAI systems test all three because "the model" is now a model plus prompts plus retrieval plus tools.

**Q23. Is an LLM-based agent a "model" under model risk management?**

Yes, and more than one. SS1/23 defines a model broadly as a quantitative method that processes inputs into outputs using assumptions, and a bank's inventory should treat the agentic system as a model with components: the foundation model, the prompts, the retrieval corpus and index, the tools and the orchestration logic. The practical approach is to tier by materiality and use, validate the system end to end on a golden set rather than attempting to validate the foundation model's internals, and put change control on every component, because a prompt edit or a corpus update changes behaviour as surely as a parameter change. Independent validation should test accuracy, groundedness, robustness to adversarial inputs, and the effectiveness of guardrails and human oversight. Treat vendor model updates as model changes that trigger re-validation. This is where many GenAI programmes stall in banks, so plan validation effort and timeline into the roadmap from day one.

**Q24. How do you test an AI system for bias in a lending context?**

Start from the protected characteristics and proxies relevant to the jurisdiction, then test outcomes, not intentions. Measure disparate impact: compare approval rates, pricing and error rates across groups; the four-fifths rule from US employment law [PUBLIC — EEOC Uniform Guidelines, 1978] is a common screening heuristic, where a selection rate below 80% of the most favoured group's rate triggers scrutiny. Test for proxy discrimination by checking whether features correlate with protected attributes, including in embeddings and text features for LLM-based systems. Examine calibration and error-rate parity, since equal approval rates can hide unequal false-negative rates. For generative components, test for differential quality of explanations or recommendations across groups with templated inputs. Document the trade-offs, because fairness metrics conflict and a choice must be made and recorded. The Apple Card episode [PUBLIC — Nov 2019; NYDFS report, Mar 2021] showed that even without a finding of unlawful discrimination, inability to explain outcomes is itself a conduct and reputational failure.

**Q25. What does the RBI's FREE-AI framework ask of Indian financial institutions?**

The Reserve Bank of India constituted the FREE-AI committee in December 2024 and published its report in August 2025 [PUBLIC — RBI, 2025; VERIFY exact wording and implementation status]. It sets out seven guiding "sutras": trust is the foundation; people first; innovation over restraint; fairness and equity; accountability; understandable by design; and safety, resilience and sustainability, and makes 26 recommendations across six pillars spanning infrastructure, policy, capacity, governance, protection and assurance. The posture is notably pro-innovation compared with the EU, with proposals such as shared data and compute infrastructure and a tolerant approach to first-mover errors, alongside board-level accountability, AI policies, incident reporting and consumer protection. For a consultant, it means Indian banks will be expected to have board-approved AI policies, inventories and grievance mechanisms, and that explainability and accountability will be examined. Treat it as direction of travel and check what has been converted into binding regulation.

### Theme 6: Delivery and operating model

**Q26. Why do AI pilots stall, and what do you do about it?**

Pilots stall because they are designed to prove the technology rather than to reach production. Causes, in rough order of frequency: no production owner or budget beyond the pilot; data access granted for a sandbox but not for production; integration never scoped; model risk validation not planned; success criteria technical rather than business; and change capacity absent, so the pilot works and nobody uses it. The statistics are stark: Gartner projected at least 30% of GenAI projects abandoned after proof of concept by end 2025 [PUBLIC — Gartner, July 2024; VERIFY], and the MIT NANDA report claimed around 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots showed no measurable P&L impact [PUBLIC — MIT NANDA, Aug 2025; VERIFY, methodology contested]. The remedy is to decide the production path before the pilot starts: owner, funding, integration budget, validation plan, exit criteria including business metrics, and a kill decision date. A pilot without a kill date is a hobby.

**Q27. Compare the operating-model archetypes for AI.**

Centralised centre of excellence: one team owns strategy, platform and delivery; fast to stand up, builds deep expertise, but becomes a bottleneck and distant from the business. Hub-and-spoke: a central hub owns platform, standards, risk and scarce skills; spokes in business units own use cases and adoption; the most common target state for banks because it balances control and proximity. Federated: business units own everything with light central coordination; speeds local innovation and fragments standards, risk and spend. Embedded: AI roles sit permanently in product teams; the end state for digitally mature organisations. McKinsey and QuantumBlack research has reported hub-and-spoke as the most common high-performer configuration [PUBLIC — McKinsey; VERIFY specific publication]. The evolution is usually CoE to hub-and-spoke to embedded as maturity grows. The decision criteria are regulatory exposure, talent scarcity, platform reuse and the speed of business demand. Whatever the archetype, risk and validation must have an independent line.

**Q28. What roles does an AI programme need, and in what ratio?**

A delivery squad for an agentic use case typically needs an AI product owner from the business, an agent or prompt engineer, one or two software engineers for integration, a data engineer, an ML or evaluation engineer, a domain SME at least half-time, and access to AI risk and validation, security and change management. Ratios [ESTIMATE — practitioner observation]: roughly one product owner and one SME per squad, two to three engineers per agent engineer, and one validation resource per two or three squads in regulated settings. Platform roles, LLMOps, observability, model gateway and guardrail services, sit centrally. The under-resourced roles are almost always evaluation engineering and change management. The mistake is to staff a GenAI programme like a data-science programme; agentic systems are integration and process-change heavy, which the 10-20-70 heuristic [PUBLIC — BCG] captures.

**Q29. How do you design KPIs for an AI deployment?**

Build a KPI tree with three layers and a causal chain between them. Model KPIs: accuracy, groundedness, false-positive and false-negative rates, latency, cost per task, human-override rate. Operational KPIs: cycle time, throughput per FTE, rework rate, queue age, exception volume. Business KPIs: unit cost, loss rate, revenue or retention, customer satisfaction, regulatory findings. Distinguish leading from lagging: override rate and adoption lead unit cost by months. Set targets at three, six and twelve months, baseline every metric before go-live, and define the measurement method and counterfactual. Watch for pitfalls: Hawthorne effects in pilots, baseline drift when volumes change, and attribution when several programmes touch the same process. The most useful single KPI for an agentic system in early life is the override rate by reason, because it tells you simultaneously about model quality, trust calibration and where to move the autonomy dial.

**Q30. How do you manage adoption and the workforce impact?**

Treat adoption as a designed outcome, not a hoped-for one. Use a change framework, Kotter's eight steps [PUBLIC — Kotter, 1996] for leadership and ADKAR from Prosci [PUBLIC — Hiatt, 2003] for individual change, and add three AI-specific elements. Trust calibration: train people to know when the system is likely wrong, because both over-trust, automation bias, and under-trust destroy value. Role redesign: specify what the human does after automation, exception handling, quality assurance, customer judgement, and pay and grade accordingly. Workforce transition: be explicit about whether the case assumes attrition, redeployment or redundancy, and plan reskilling early. The Commonwealth Bank of Australia case in 2025, where redundancies attributed to an AI voice bot were reversed after volumes did not fall as assumed [PUBLIC — Aug 2025; VERIFY], shows what happens when headcount is removed on a modelled rather than measured effect. Measure adoption weekly in the first quarter.

### Theme 7: Industry and cases

**Q31. Where does AI create the most value in a bank today?**

Three clusters, in order of proven value. Operations with high volumes of document- and text-heavy work under codified rules: KYC and KYB, AML alert triage, sanctions adjudication, disputes, payment investigations, reconciliation breaks. Here agentic and GenAI patterns compress cycle time and cost and the KPIs are measurable. Risk and fraud, where machine learning has been delivering for a decade and GenAI adds narrative generation and investigator assistance; Mastercard reported an average 20% fraud-detection uplift from generative techniques in 2024 [PUBLIC — Mastercard, Feb 2024; VERIFY]. Knowledge work and servicing: adviser assistants, contact-centre copilots and coding assistants, where value is real but diffuse and hard to convert to cost. The McKinsey Global Institute estimated USD 200–340 billion of annual value for banking from generative AI [PUBLIC — MGI, June 2023]. My view for a client: fund operations use cases for cost and cycle time, risk for loss reduction, and treat knowledge-worker tools as a productivity floor rather than a business case.

**Q32. What do you take from the Klarna case?**

Klarna announced in February 2024 that its OpenAI-based assistant handled two-thirds of customer-service chats in its first month, equivalent to about 700 full-time agents, with an estimated USD 40 million profit improvement [PUBLIC — Klarna, Feb 2024]. In 2025 the CEO publicly acknowledged the company had over-indexed on cost and would re-hire humans to restore service quality [PUBLIC — interviews, May 2025; VERIFY]. Three lessons. Scope automation by error tolerance and customer impact, not by cost ambition; the reversal was about quality on the cases the assistant should never have fully owned. Keep human capacity as a designed tier, not a residual. And be careful with early claims: first-month resolution statistics measure deflection, not resolution quality, and the market now discounts them. For a consultant, it is the best public illustration of why human-in-the-loop design is an economic decision as well as a risk one.

**Q33. What did HSBC do with AI in anti-money laundering, and why does it matter?**

HSBC worked with Google Cloud on AI-based transaction monitoring, the "Dynamic Risk Assessment" approach later productised as Google's AML AI. Google Cloud reported that the system identified two to four times more suspicious activity while cutting alert volumes by around 60% [PUBLIC — Google Cloud, June 2023; VERIFY]. It matters for three reasons. It is a machine-learning risk-scoring case, not a GenAI case, which is a useful corrective when a client assumes AI means chatbots. It shows the dual benefit shape regulators care about: fewer false positives and more true positives at the same time, which is the only way to justify replacing rules-based monitoring to a supervisor. And it is a multi-year, bank-and-hyperscaler build, not a procurement, which tells a client what commitment looks like. In an AML conversation I use it to frame the ambition and then immediately caveat that the regulator must accept the model under the bank's MRM framework.

**Q34. Tell me about an AI failure in financial services and what it teaches.**

Two I use. Zillow Offers [PUBLIC — Nov 2021]: an algorithmic home-buying business wound down after pricing-model losses of more than USD 500 million and a roughly 25% workforce cut. The lesson is that a model's error distribution matters more than its average accuracy when the model is exposed to a market with adverse selection; sellers accepted offers when the model over-priced. The Apple Card episode [PUBLIC — Nov 2019; NYDFS Mar 2021]: allegations of gender bias in credit limits; the regulator found no unlawful discrimination but criticised the inability to explain decisions to customers. The lesson is that explainability is a conduct requirement independent of the fairness finding. For agentic systems I add Air Canada [PUBLIC — Feb 2024], where a tribunal held the airline liable for its chatbot's incorrect fare statement: the organisation owns what its AI says. Each one maps to a design control: error-cost analysis, explanation capability, and output guardrails with human accountability.

**Q35. How do you apply AI in payments operations specifically?**

Payments operations is a strong fit because the data is structured, the rules are codified and the volumes are high. Four use cases. Exceptions and repairs: ISO 20022 structured party and remittance data lets models repair and route exceptions with far higher precision than free-text MT messages allowed; agents can assemble the fix and a human releases high-value items. Investigations: cross-border inquiries are text-heavy, multi-party and SLA-bound, an ideal orchestrated-agent domain with retrieval over scheme and correspondent rules. Fraud and authorised push payment scams in real-time rails: the sub-second window excludes heavy models from the authorisation path, so ML scoring sits in-line and GenAI sits in pre-transaction customer warnings and post-transaction investigation. Reconciliation: pattern-matching for breaks with agentic root-cause narratives. The strategic point: ISO 20022 migration is the data foundation most AI assessments in payments under-weight; banks that populated the structured fields have a feasibility advantage.

### Theme 8: Personal and behavioural

**Q36. Why AI strategy consulting, and why now?**

Give a reason tied to your trajectory and the market, not enthusiasm for technology. "I have spent eight years in financial services consulting, mostly payments, and the last two watching AI move from analytics into the operating core of the processes I know: onboarding, disputes, screening. The scarce skill in this market is not building models; it is knowing a bank's processes and data well enough to decide where AI belongs and defending that to a COO, a CFO and a model risk committee. That is the intersection I sit at. Now matters because banks are moving from pilots to scaled programmes, and that transition is where strategy consultants earn their fee: scoping, business case, operating model and governance. I want to lead that work, and this practice is where that work is being done at the level I aspire to."

**Q37. Tell me about a time you changed a client's or team's mind with analysis.**

Use the underwriting diagnostic. "The working hypothesis was that AI would draft credit memos. My SME interviews and time analysis showed the dominant pain point was information assembly across disconnected systems, not analysis. I presented the heatmap and argued that the proposition should be orchestrated assembly with the analyst owning judgement, which also kept us clear of autonomous credit decisioning and its regulatory classification. The team re-scoped. What made it land was that the recommendation reduced regulatory exposure and increased value at the same time, so it had no natural opponent. What I would do differently is quantify in the interviews rather than afterwards." Keep it to 90 seconds; name the decision, the evidence and the outcome.

**Q38. Describe a failure and what you learned.**

Use the prototype. "I built the dispute prototype before I built its evaluation set. The architecture worked on the scenarios I had in mind and then produced confident but wrong outcomes on edge cases I had not written down, including a broad agent attempting actions outside its remit. I retrofitted a golden set and narrowed each agent's tools and prompt. The lesson I carry into every engagement now is that the evaluation set is the specification: you define what correct looks like, with coverage of the ugly cases, before you build or buy anything. It also changed how I write business cases: accuracy on the golden set at the chosen autonomy level is now an explicit assumption, not an afterthought."

**Q39. How do you handle a client stakeholder who wants to automate everything?**

"I take the ambition seriously and redirect it to where it survives. First I agree the goal, usually unit cost or cycle time, and then I show the task-level scoring: which steps are deterministic and belong in rules, which are judgement-heavy and agent-fit, and which are irreversible and stay human. I put a number on the automatable share and a number on the cost of being wrong at each step. Then I show the Klarna arc [PUBLIC — 2024–25; VERIFY] as what over-reach looks like in public. Most sponsors want the outcome, not the autonomy level; once they see that a hybrid design delivers most of the value with a fraction of the risk, the conversation moves to sequencing. If they still insist, I document the risk acceptance explicitly and make sure risk and compliance are in the room."

**Q40. Where do you see agentic AI in banking in three years, and what would you tell a CEO to do now?**

"Three years out I expect agentic systems to run the evidence-gathering and drafting layers of most operations processes, with humans owning the consequential decisions, and I expect regulators to have settled what validation of an agentic system means. I do not expect autonomous decisioning in credit or adverse customer outcomes in that window; the regulatory and liability picture argues against it. I would tell a CEO three things. Fix the data and integration foundations now, because they gate everything and take longest. Build the model-risk and evaluation capability for probabilistic systems, because validation, not technology, is the bottleneck in banks. And pick two or three processes to reshape end to end rather than fifty pilots, with a production owner and a kill date for each. The value is in reshaping, not deploying tools, and the organisations that will lead are those that treat AI as a process-redesign programme with a technology component."
