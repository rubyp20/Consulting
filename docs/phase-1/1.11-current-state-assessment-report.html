Part A · Executive summary

The situation

Business Bank is replacing the Business Lending Platform. The current-state maps produced for the replacement describe a process built around eleven deal types, each with its own journey, and the risk is that the new platform is configured to reproduce it. This assessment establishes, from the Bank’s own maps and operating data, what the process actually is, what it costs the Bank in time and effort, where the controls sit, and what should change before the replacement is designed.

What we found

1. It is one process, not eleven. Across the nine lending journeys, the first five steps are identical, the decision flow is 82% common, and the completion flow is 76% common. The differences are parameters (which product, which authority level, which items in the submission) and three genuine flow variants (distressed connections, facility removal, renewals without security). Eleven deal types resolve to five journey patterns; three of the eleven carry no live customer process at all.

2. The work is servicing, and servicing is processed as if it were origination. Excluding records migration, renewals and variations are 91% of live deals. A renewal, which by definition involves no structural change, passes through every decision step a new-money deal does: analysis, validation, document check, authority check, Credit assessment. The Bank already operates a risk-based fast-track for pre-approved drawdowns; it has never applied the same logic to its largest volume.

3. Time is lost waiting, not working. Every stage boundary is a hand-off by message, report or email; no stage has a timer, SLA or queue view. The facility-letter stage, where the RM emails a PDF and waits, is the longest single wait and the only one with a control: a three-month reset that sends the deal back to pre-credit. Two unbounded rework loops leave Credit on every journey.

4. Controls are mostly sound in intent and weak in execution. Of 28 controls inventoried, four are regulatory and must not change; about a fifth have no documented source. The most consequential, delegated-authority determination, is performed by hand against a document, although every input is in the system. Nine controls would be stronger if automated. The main design constraint is not removing controls; it is expressing them as rules.

5. The workarounds are the requirements. Four batch jobs, a set of bots, off-platform letters and memos, and four deal-type field combinations each cover a capability the system lacks: timers, queues, documents, rules, integrations, ownership. Twenty-three workarounds trace to seven root causes, four of which are standard capability in current lending platforms.

6. The data that feeds regulatory reporting is not documented at field level. Forbearance, impairment inputs, exposure, limits and risk grade all originate in process steps the redesign will touch. A protected-fields list has been drafted and must be agreed before any configuration decision.

2. The process as it is

2.1 Scale and shape

30,561 deals in twelve months; 26,745 once ESR Cleanse (records migration recorded as deals) is excluded. On that base, Renewal and EPR are 55.6%, Variation 27.2%, New and Increase 11.3%, distressed-connection deals 5.7%, pre-approved drawdowns 0.2%. Two deal types have no live process (Staged Drawdown as an origination type, with zero deals; DO NOT USE) and two live journeys have no type of their own (facility removal; staged-drawdown allocation, recorded through a field combination).

3. Findings

3.1 Over-processing of servicing

The renewal decision flow is step-for-step the same as new money (1.3 §2, rows 2.01–2.18). “Credit analytics if required” on renewals and variations has no stated criterion. Extension Pending Review, intended as a short-term bridge, forms chains of up to three before a renewal that happens anyway. Variations are not triaged: a covenant-date change and a security restructure follow the same path. Meanwhile the Bank’s pre-approved drawdown journeys show that it already knows how to route by prior approval and refer to Credit only on material change.

3.2 Waiting and rework

No stage in the lifecycle has a timer, SLA, queue view or escalation. Renewals are triggered by a report, not a task. Hand-offs to Credit, Lending Services and Fulfilment leave the system (message, email, report), so queue time is invisible to the RM and the customer. Two loops leave Credit on every journey (deferred for information; declined) with no bound on count or time. The facility letter is generated by BLP and then issued by the RM as a PDF; sent and returned status is not captured; a letter unreturned at three months resets the deal to pre-credit. For two products the letter is produced outside the platform altogether.

3.3 Controls

Twenty-eight controls were inventoried (1.6). Four are regulatory (forbearance assessment; ERV, provisions and write-offs; insider-information access; KYC identification in part) and are retained unchanged in every option. Sixteen trace, on current evidence, to credit policy. Six have no documented source other than practice or system limitation, including the RM pre-screen, deal validation, post-approval materiality and the reallocation arithmetic. Twenty-four are performed manually; nine are candidates for automation, and six of those become stronger controls when automated, with delegated-authority determination the most consequential. The design question for Phase 2 is equivalence, not removal.

3.4 Workarounds

Twenty-three workarounds (1.7) trace to seven root causes: no timers; no cross-team work management; no document generation, delivery or signature; no rules engine; missing integrations (customer, balances, fees, bureau); a rigid deal-type model; and no audited ownership transfer. Four of the seven are standard capability in current lending platforms; one is Programme integration scope; two are design decisions this project owns. Thirteen workarounds are expected to be met natively, five retired by design, four depend on integration, and the bots are dispositioned individually once specified.

3.5 Data

BLP is a stated source for regulatory reporting, but lineage from report line to BLP field is not documented; MI relies on manual adjustment, with the deal-type model the likely main cause. Fifteen field groups have been mapped to the process steps that create them (1.8 §5). Five of the fifteen are rated high redesign risk because levers in the long-list touch them: fast-track renewals (risk grade refresh), retiring ESR Cleanse (exposure updates), retiring batch jobs (forbearance status), capture-once and on-platform documents (classification and recovery fields). A draft protected-fields list at four protection levels is the first dependency the Replacement Programme receives (B-07).

3.6 Pain points and root causes

Forty-three pain points (1.9) were identified from the maps, inventories and RFP using three lenses and a fixed question bank; frequency and impact bands are confirmed in workshops, interviews and survey. The top fifteen carry 63% of the indicative score. Ten root-cause themes consolidate to six underlying causes, and four of those six drive fourteen of the top fifteen pain points:

6.1 Implications for the Phase 2 design

  1. Design principles follow from U1–U6 and are proposed for Design Authority sign-off in Week 8 before any map is drawn.
  2. Stage 1 is designed once, Stage 2 twice (lending; distressed), Stage 3 once with three gates. Deal type becomes a parameter; controls become a rules table per pattern × risk band.
  3. The control equivalence register leads with the six controls that get stronger, then the three that need second-line judgement, and leaves the three policy questions to Credit Risk Policy.
  4. The protected-fields list is a constraint on every configuration decision; levers touching P1 fields carry a stated design obligation.
  5. Fit-to-standard: thirteen workarounds are expected to be met natively; the custom list starts with the four integration items and whatever OP-15 needs that the platform lacks.
  6. Interim design release at Week 10 carries the deal-type model, deal-start component, routing concept, letter approach and retired workarounds, so the Programme can proceed if its freeze precedes G2.
